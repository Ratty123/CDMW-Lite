# Changelog

Notable changes to CDMW Archive Lite are recorded here. The format follows [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/), and package versions follow semantic versioning.

## [Unreleased]

### Added

- A character mesh exports with its skeleton and its weights, as a rigged GLB and a rigged FBX. Until now a `.pac` arrived in Blender as an unposable shell: nothing in the export path read skinning at all.

  The binding was in the vertex records the whole time, and it is six influences per vertex rather than the four an earlier reading took the field for. Bytes 20 to 27 are two `u32` holding three 10-bit palette slots each, and bytes 28 to 33 are six `u8` weights that descend and sum to 255 give or take rounding. Reading the field as four `u8` slots is what used to pair a forehead bone with a forearm, because influence 1's index straddles bytes 21 and 22. Measured on a full character body, 13,740 vertices over three submeshes: the weights sum to 255 ± 2 on every record, they descend on every record, and the influence counts split 2,145 / 2,869 / 2,851 / 2,284 / 1,830 / 1,761 across one through six.

  A slot is not a bone index. It indexes the `.pac`'s own palette of `.pab` bone-name hashes, which is recovered by generating every byte run that fits the shape and keeping the one whose hashes all resolve against a skeleton — of the thousands of runs that merely look like a palette in a body mesh, exactly one resolves. The file's head is searched first, where a character body keeps its palette, and the whole file after, which is where a coat or a boot keeps it.

  The `.pab` skeleton is read at its documented fixed layout and nothing is guessed: a record that does not fit yields no skeleton rather than a scanned approximation of one, because a bone recovered by scanning is named and placed on evidence the file did not give. Chaining each bone's stored local transform through the hierarchy reproduces the bind matrix the file also stores, to within 3.4e-6 across a 448-bone rig, which is what lets the same data serve glTF's nodes and FBX's LimbNodes.

  Verified by posing the result in Blender rather than by inspecting the file. A body imports with 249 joints over 13,740 vertices, no unweighted vertex, and no vertex whose weights miss 1.0 by more than 5.4e-08; rotating the right upper arm 45 degrees moves 3,153 vertices at a mean distance of 0.490 from that bone against 0.730 for the vertices that stay put, and the right hand moves 1,983 at 0.099 against 0.681. Posing the right and left upper arms moves 3,153 vertices each, on opposite sides of the body and no others. The two formats agree to the digit on every count and distance, which is the check that they bind the same vertices to the same bones.

  The GLB carries the bones the mesh uses plus the ancestors that place them, 249 of a 448-bone rig, because the rest are joints nothing moves. The FBX carries the whole skeleton, because FBX is the format an animation clip is brought back in through and a clip addresses bones a given garment is not weighted to. `scripts/blender/` holds the verification script and the reference numbers.

  A rigidly bound mesh — a prop, an accessory, a vehicle — is told apart from a smooth-skinned one by its records: every vertex is a single influence at full weight and every slot is zero. Those files carry no bone hash anywhere, so the bone they follow is recorded outside the mesh and there is nothing to resolve. They export unrigged, as they always have, and the package says which case it found rather than reporting a failure. Of a random sweep of 23 character meshes, 10 exported rigged, 1 was rigid, and 12 were smooth-skinned with a palette no rig their own name or directory nominates accounts for; those also export unrigged, because an NPC upper body's 88-entry palette resolves completely against nine different humanoid rigs and a monster's against ten, so once the rig is not nominated by name "the palette resolves" stops identifying which rig it is.

- Item Finder shows an item's in-game description. Every item record carries two localization keys, one for the name shown in the inventory and one for the text under it, and until now nothing here read the second. It appears in the detail pane under the item's name; 3,730 of the 3,789 listed items have one. Descriptions are deliberately left out of the search text, so a search for `plate` still returns the plate armour rather than everything whose description mentions it.

- Item Finder shows what the item record says about the item: its equip type, its grade, and how many of it fit in one inventory slot. An item row is a serialized C++ struct with no schema anywhere in the archive, so these three fields were recovered by differencing rows against each other and then checked against something outside the table before being shown.

  The equip type is a key into `equiptypeinfo`, which names all 113 of the game's types itself: `Helm`, `Upperbody`, `OneHandSword`, `HorseSaddle`. It is stated by 2,997 of the listed items, and it is right: every `OneHandSword` is an item whose own name ends `_OneHandSword`, every `Upperbody` is a chest piece, every `HorseSaddle` a saddle, across all 88 types the shipped items use. It is searchable as well as shown, so `Helm` finds the helmets.

  The grade is a single byte reading 0 to 6, stated by 1,051 items and absent from the rest. Its ladder is confirmed from outside the table: the share of items named `Legendary_*` climbs with it, from 0.8% at grade 0 to 20.5% at grade 5.

  The stack size is the first number after the item's internal name, and it reads 1 for weapons and armour, 50 for cannonballs, 100 for arrows, 100,000 for copper. It is also, as it turns out, exactly what the old pattern scan was keying off without knowing it: the byte run it searched for is this field holding 1, which is why the items it could see were almost exactly the items that do not stack.

  A row that does not state one of these shows nothing for it rather than a zero, and a value outside the range the field is known to take is dropped rather than displayed. No other item field is claimed: the rest of the record holds variable-length lists that move everything after them, and guessing at those would put numbers on screen that nobody has checked.

### Changed

- **An exported FBX now states that its geometry is in metres, which changes the size of every FBX this application writes.** If you have been scaling Archive Lite's FBX imports up by 100, or working at a hundredth scale, stop: they now arrive at their true size. GLB and OBJ are unaffected and always were.

  The writer declared `UnitScaleFactor` 1. That field says how many centimetres one unit is and an importer divides by it — Blender computes its global scale as `UnitScaleFactor / 100` — so declaring 1 claimed centimetres for geometry that is in metres, and every FBX arrived at a hundredth of its size. A character body imported 18 mm tall. The file loaded, the rig worked, and the answer was quietly wrong, which is why it lasted: nothing refuses a mesh for being small.

  The declaration is now 100, with `OriginalUnitScaleFactor` beside it. The geometry itself is untouched: reimporting the old and the new file and scaling the old by a hundred puts every one of 180,325 vertices within 1.4e-7 of its counterpart, which is `float32` rounding and nothing else. Blender now reads both formats at the same size — a character body measures 1.8394 tall out of the GLB and out of the FBX, and the same to four decimals for a legwear, an upperbody and an accessory. The focused gate pins the declaration in the rigged and the unrigged FBX both, because nothing else in the suite would notice it changing.

### Removed

- Item Finder no longer shows or filters on material tags. The tag was never consistent enough to filter by, and it did not say what it appeared to say. It was read from `partprefabdyeslotinfo`, the part prefab dye slot table, which records which zones of a model are tintable rather than what an item is made of, and it was keyed by model, so it described the mesh and not the item. `Elish Leather Boots` came up `metal`, and so did `Bolton Leather Boots`, because both are the same `cd_phm_00_foot_0110.pac` and any two items sharing a model were given identical tags by construction. `Benberry Leather Boots` came up with nothing at all, being the `_index06` variant of that same model: the variant stripper knew `_index01` through `_index03` and the archive numbers variants to `_index21`, so the lookup missed. That accounts for 224 of the untagged items, 57 of which have a stripped root that is tagged.

  The signal underneath was real but partial. 580 of the 3,594 catalog items carried any tag at all, and the reader recognised twenty English keywords by substring, first match winning per string, so a slot named outside that set produced nothing and a slot naming two produced only the first. Nothing verified which model a slot name belonged to either: the table was read by scanning for length-prefixed strings and attaching each run of them to the next model path that appeared, with no record boundaries and no row directory, which is the one table here still read that way.

  Making this trustworthy means reading the dye slot table through its own `.pabgh` row directory and then mapping the game's slot vocabulary onto something a person would call a material, and every item in the archive would have to be checked before the filter could be believed. That is a lot of work for a facet whose answer mostly restated the item's own name, so it is gone rather than half-right. The accelerator no longer reads `partprefabdyeslotinfo` at all, tag words no longer feed the model search aliases, and cached catalogs rebuild on first open.

### Fixed

- Item Finder can reach every item the game defines, rather than the 63.6% of them it could see. It had been locating item records by searching `iteminfo.pabgb` for a fourteen-byte pattern, but that pattern is not a record header. It is the tail of the first sub-record inside a record, preceded by a scalar field, and it only forms when that field happens to hold the value 1. Records whose field holds anything else, which is 2,366 of the table's 6,508 rows, were invisible: the very first two rows in the table, `Pyeonjeon_Arrow` and `Arrow`, both hold 100 there and neither was ever found. The table ships beside a companion `iteminfo.pabgh` that is its row directory, holding a row count and then each row's primary key with that row's byte offset into the blob, which gives exact bounds for all 6,508 rows and needs no searching at all. Widths are not declared in the pair, so they are recovered by search and then checked against the payload the directory claims to describe: offsets start at zero, rise strictly, stay inside the blob, and every row repeats its own key at its first byte. `stringinfo.pabgb` is read through its own directory the same way. Item Finder now lists 3,789 items where it listed 3,141. An archive that ships no usable directory still builds a catalog by the old scan, but the result now says so rather than passing a short catalog off as a complete one.

- Nine model links Item Finder used to draw were wrong and are gone. Having no record boundaries, the scan looked for icon references up to 2,500 bytes past its anchor, which reaches well into the records that follow: `Trap Bomb` was taking its model from rows belonging to `Crow Bomb`, `Water Bomb` and `Vine Bomb`, three to seven records away, and `Layta Plate Greaves` from `Batz Plate Boots` next door. Reading the row's real extent cannot make that mistake. This is the Lite half of a coordinated change. CDMW Full carries a byte-identical copy of the old scanner and is being corrected to match, so that both read the same 6,508 rows rather than agreeing only in being wrong the same way.

- The game's string table is parsed rather than sifted. `localizationstring_eng.paloc` was read by stepping one byte at a time looking for anything that resembled a length-prefixed string, and accepting a key only if it was six to twenty ASCII digits. That recovered 132,171 of the file's 187,521 strings and could not, by construction, reach the 54,787 whose keys are not numeric: every `questdialog`, `aidialogstringinfogroup`, `node`, `textdialog` and `quest` entry in the game. The format is a flat run of `{category, reserved, key length, key, text length, text}` records closed by a four-byte record count; reading it that way recovers all 187,521 and steps record to record instead of byte to byte, which measured about an order of magnitude cheaper over the shipped 16 MB table. Two things are checked rather than assumed, because a string table that is quietly short looks the same as a game that ships fewer strings: the walk has to land exactly on the footer, and the records walked have to equal the count the footer declares. A file failing either is rejected whole and named in the result. The category each string is filed under is carried through, which is the cheapest way to tell a quest line from an item name; every item name and description in the shipped archive is category 7. This did not change what Item Finder shows, and was never going to: all 6,508 item keys are numeric, so the digit filter passed them.

- An exported mesh carries the archive's own vertices, in the archive's own order, so it can take a shape key. Rebuilding the index buffer had been done by rejoining corners whose position, normal and texture coordinate matched, which recovers the right points but numbers them in the order the triangles happen to reach them: on a character head, exported vertex 0 was source vertex 2,945 and vertex 1 was source vertex 3,037. Every per-vertex correspondence — a shape key, a morph target, a transferred weight — is matched by index, so a mesh exported here and a mesh exported by CDMW Full held the same points under different numbers, and joining one to the other as a shape blew the model apart. Matching on attributes also folded together source vertices that agreed on all three, losing 2 of one submesh's 2,281 vertices and 27 of another's 5,302, which the archive had kept apart. The preview package records which source vertex every corner was copied from, and that record now fixes both the count and the order, for OBJ, FBX and GLB alike. Two exports of related meshes therefore line up with each other and with CDMW Full's.

- An exported OBJ names each part after the part, not after its material. The `o` line was written from the material name, so parts sharing one material arrived under one name and stopped being tellable apart — a head's left and right eye both landing as `CD_PHM_00_Eye_0001`, where CDMW Full writes `CD_PGW_00_Head_Sub_00_0001_EyeLeft_0001` and `..._EyeRight_0001`. The submesh's own name is now used for the object and the material name is kept for `usemtl`, which is the split CDMW Full has always written.

- An exported mesh is now the same file CDMW Full writes, byte for byte. The last two differences were both the same mistake: the export was reading the copy of the geometry the preview window submits to the graphics card. That copy is decoded in single precision and framed into a display cube, so every coordinate disagreed with Full's in the eighth digit once the framing was undone; and its normals are scaled to unit length for shading, where the source record decodes to lengths from 0.13 to 1.41 and Full states what the record holds. The parser now sets down the vertices an interchange file needs as it reads them — position, normal and texture coordinate in double, in source space, in the source's own order — and the export takes those. On a full character body, all 13,740 positions, all 13,740 normals, all 13,740 texture coordinates and all 25,158 faces are identical to Full's, and so are the material library and the round-trip sidecar. The only line that still differs is the `source_path` comment, which states where each tool read the model from and is a fact about the input rather than the output. Blender was asked directly: importing both files and joining one onto the other as a shape key moves no vertex at all, on any of the three submeshes.

- A PAMLOD is read the way CDMW Full reads it, so an exported LOD is the same mesh in both. The two had been finding the LOD entry table by different means -- Full locates each entry by its texture name and takes the counts and offsets from fixed positions around the string; Lite walked the table at a fixed stride -- and then searched different ranges for the vertex block. They landed on different strides for the same file, which is a disagreement about the format rather than about exporting, and it put every coordinate somewhere else: one proxy LOD came out with 330,439 vertices where Full read 198,452. Full's reading is now tried first and Lite's own search still stands behind it for a file that one cannot read. On that proxy LOD all 198,452 positions and all 196,012 faces now match Full's exactly.

- A PAM or PAMLOD export names its parts the way CDMW Full names them, and states texture coordinates only where the source has any. A PAM part is known to the preview by its texture, because that is what resolves its material, but Full calls it `mesh_07_cd_akapen_wall_plaster_04` and an interchange file has to agree with Full; both names are kept now, each used where it belongs. A LOD group takes the zero-padded form and its first entry's material rather than that entry's texture. Where the source's vertex records are too narrow to hold a texture coordinate, none is written, instead of a UV of zero per vertex claiming the source stated one. A LOD group is also numbered along its own combined array rather than by source vertex, because its entries take overlapping slices of one shared buffer and numbering by source folded distinct vertices together: one proxy LOD lost 310,022 of its 330,439 vertices that way.

- An exported OBJ's material library binds a texture again. `map_Kd` was written only when a texture happened to be exported alongside, so a mesh exported on its own named materials that pointed at nothing. The source's own texture name is stated now, as CDMW Full states it, with the extension the archive stores these under when the name carries none. A texture actually exported beside the mesh still wins, bound by its real relative path. The round-trip sidecar names it too, which it had been leaving empty.

- A PAC descriptor keeps the name the file gives it rather than a placeholder. Current equipment PACs store one shared name and material record terminated by a NUL, and the scanner here only knew the older layout of two adjacent records with no terminator. The NUL is not printable, so both passes rejected the record that was there to be read and named the part `unknown_133` or `unknown_196` — names that reached the preview, the material binding and any file exported from either. The terminated form is decoded first, as CDMW Full decodes it, and the two now agree across every case the layouts admit. Cached preview packages keep the names they were built with, so the package version is raised to rebuild them.

## [1.0.2] - 2026-07-31

### Added

- Any export can bring the focused file's family with it, through an `Include family` tick in the export dialog. A mesh export puts the related materials, textures, descriptors and other resolved assets in a `referenced_files/` folder beside it, and a folder export writes them alongside the selection under the same layout. An OBJ binds any texture that arrives this way as its material's `map_Kd`.

### Fixed

- An exported mesh is a mesh again rather than a heap of loose triangles. The export was written from the copy the preview window submits to the graphics card, which holds three unshared vertices for every triangle because the index buffer has already been spent on it. Nothing in the result was joined to anything else: it rendered correctly and could not be edited, having no edge loops, nothing to select as linked and no clean way to subdivide, and it was several times larger than it needed to be — a head that indexes 13,740 vertices shipped 75,474. The index buffer is now rebuilt for OBJ, FBX and GLB alike by rejoining corners that came from one vertex, which is exact: they were copied from a single source record, so a seam or a hard edge the source authored differs in its texture coordinate or its normal and stays apart.

- An exported OBJ names materials that exist. It had always written a `usemtl` line per submesh while writing no material library, so every part of the model arrived untextured and indistinguishable. The library is now written beside it, along with the round-trip sidecar that records which archive entry the mesh came from.

- The window reopens at the size and place it was left, on any display. Its remembered position was kept in units that only mean something next to one monitor's scale, so on a desktop mixing scales it came back enlarged by the difference between them and hung off the edges of the screen. It is now kept in physical pixels and checked against the displays actually attached, so it survives a monitor being rescaled, resized, rearranged or unplugged. A position remembered by an earlier build is not carried over, because the scale its numbers were written against was never recorded.

- An exported mesh keeps the position and size the archive gives it. The exporter had been pointed at the wrong one of two similar-looking sets of geometry — a mixup on my part between the model's own vertex data and the copy prepared for the preview window. The preview's copy is deliberately altered so the camera can frame any asset the same way: every position is recentred on the model's bounding box and rescaled into a two-unit cube. A character head, 17 cm across and sitting 1.71 m up the skeleton, therefore arrived in Blender as a 2-unit head at the origin. OBJ, FBX and GLB were all written from that copy, so all three lost the same thing. The transform the preview applied is recorded alongside it, so the export path now undoes it and writes the coordinates the source actually holds. Normals and UVs were never affected — a uniform recentre and rescale leaves them as they were. The recorded transform is also now stated precisely enough to invert cleanly: at the six significant digits it was written with, a model far from the origin has its centre rounded by more than its own size.

## [1.0.1] - 2026-07-30

### Added

- The Archive Browser has a Folders pane, a tree sitting between the filters and the file list with its own splitter, shown in the `Folders` view and replacing the flat list of every directory in the current result that used to sit inside the filter column. Each folder carries the number of files at or below it, so the shape of an archive is readable at a glance — `character (419,660)` above `motion (314,816)` — and a level is fetched only when it is expanded. The structure is derived from the same pass over the archive that builds the file list, so a search does not read it twice, and it is kept resident in the worker, which makes every later expansion a lookup. The counts follow the filters in force: a folder counts only the files that match, and one holding nothing that matches is not listed. The folder filter itself is deliberately left out, since the tree is how a folder gets chosen. Selecting a folder filters the list to it; right-clicking one exports that folder and everything beneath it with its structure preserved. The pane's width is remembered between runs, and its own `All` row is the way back to every folder.

- Right-clicking in the file list offers `Export selected`, `Export family` and `Copy file name`; right-clicking a folder in the Folders pane offers `Export folder`, the only action a folder has, since it has no family to follow and no file name to copy. The click selects the row under the cursor first, unless that row is already part of a multiple selection, which it leaves alone so the export still covers everything the user picked. The menus are drawn in the theme's own surface, border and hover colours, and a disabled entry reads as disabled rather than merely pale: a menu opens in a popup window of its own and takes none of the shell's chrome with it, so what Windows supplies by default is a light menu inside a dark shell — which is what the text fields' own editing menus had been showing.

- A Wwise sound bank plays. A `.bnk` carries its own table of the sounds it embeds, so the preview lists them — one row per sound, labelled with the Wwise source id a streamed copy would carry as its `.wem` file name — and decodes the selected one with the bundled vgmstream, the decoder that already handles a loose `.wem`. Choosing another row decodes that sound; the transport buttons and the metadata beside them then describe the sound playing, not the bank around it. A bank that carries only events keeps its readable analysis and says why: its audio streams from separate `.wem` files, so there is nothing inside it to play.

- A Wwise sound bank links the sounds that belong with it. A bank names its sounds by source id rather than by path, so nothing written inside one looks like a file name and the drawer had nothing to follow. Its media table is now read the way the preview already reads it, and each id is turned into the `.wem` name a loose copy of that sound carries, so where the archive ships one it appears under the bank in Associated assets. A bank that carries only events has no media table, so there is nothing there to link.

### Changed

- The category navigator is a checkbox of its own beside `Descending` rather than two of the `View` choices, so it can be shown alongside any arrangement of the entry list. `Categories` and `Categories + folders` leave `View`, which now offers `Folders` and `Flat`; a saved choice of either departed view reopens on the list it was paired with, with the navigator switched on. Turning the navigator on in the flat view also costs a full pass over the archive, because the counts it shows cannot come from the shortcut that view otherwise pages out of.
- The `Folder` picker is gone from Filters. The folder tree replaces it, and its own `All` row is what releases the filter. Because the pane is the only surface that reaches the folder filter and the flat view hides it, moving there releases the filter rather than leaving it applied with nothing on screen to clear it.
- A maximized window now stops at the edge of the monitor's work area instead of covering the taskbar, which had been hiding the status bar and the paging row above it. The window draws its own frame, and Windows maximizes such a window to the whole monitor unless it is told otherwise; it is told per monitor, so this holds on a multi-monitor desktop.
- The file list's header strip and paging row no longer square off the corners of the card they sit in.
- Operation progress no longer sits in a panel above the workspace. That panel appeared and disappeared with the operation, pushing the entire interface down and letting it spring back for however long the work took, which for the short ones was most of what you noticed about them. The progress text, its detail and the bar now sit in the status bar along the bottom, where the bar is shorter than the line of text beside it and so cannot move anything.
- The file list now has one `Item name` column instead of separate `Known name` and `Name evidence` columns, matching CDMW Full. The two carried the same kind of information at different confidence, and a row never filled both: an entry the archive names outright showed its name beside the literal text `Exact localization`, and an entry named only by a related model, icon, texture or sidecar showed the guess beside an empty name. The merged column shows whichever of the two a row has, and the cell's tooltip says which — the name the archive states, or a likely name that is not proof this file belongs to that item. Sorting the column orders by the merged value, so an inferred name sorts under its own name rather than as a blank; a saved `Name evidence` sort lands on the merged column, which is now the only place either name appears. Both columns were off in the shipped defaults, so the change is visible once the column is turned on under `Columns`.
- The Item Finder shows the catalog's own vocabulary in the language you selected. Its categories and groups, the material tags beside them, and the evidence lines under a selected item were the last English left in that window: they are produced in English by the classifier and the native index, and they travel back to the worker unchanged as search filters, so translating them where they are produced would break the filter round trip. The label is translated for display and the value underneath is left alone — a Korean picker reads `방어구 / 상의 (994)` and still filters on `Armor` and `Body`. All fourteen shipped languages carry the full vocabulary. A term the classifier gains later shows its English until it is given a resource string, and a test names the ones missing rather than letting a picker ship half translated.

### Fixed

- Associated assets follows every format the app recognises rather than the thirty-eight extensions its own list happened to name. The drawer kept a hand-written vocabulary while the preview pane read the shared capability manifest, so the two disagreed about what a file points at: the preview would report that a level names `world/mesh.pat` and the drawer would not list it. A mesh (`.pat`, `.patx`, `.mesh`), a surface (`.material`, `.shader`, `.mtl`), a motion (`.pai`, `.ani`), a level, route or gimmick (`.levelinfo`, `.palevel`, `.road`, `.roadsector`, `.nav`, `.pathc`, `.paccd`, `.pampg`) and the `.prefab_xml` sidecar could none of them be found as a companion or followed as a reference. All of them now can, and each lands in a group of its own instead of the `Other` heap. The vocabulary is derived from the same manifest the classifiers read, so a format registered there is linkable without anyone remembering that a second list exists.
- A reference is followed whole instead of being clipped onto the shorter registered format its name starts with. `city.paccd` was read as `city.pac` and `track.pampg` as `track.pam`, so a level's collision and gimmick data went unlinked — and where a model of that name existed, the drawer listed the model in its place with full confidence rather than admitting it had found nothing. `crate.prefab_xml` likewise resolved to `crate.prefab`, a different file of a different format.
- Opening a companion no longer leaves it showing a shallower family than the search that found it. Resolving a model's family taught every member about the others, and a member opened afterwards answered from that memory alone and never looked for its own links, so for the rest of the session whichever file was opened first was the only one with a complete answer. What the family already knows is now a head start that a member's own pass can add to.
- The Item Finder no longer probes for `.pact` when resolving a model stem. No such format is registered anywhere in the app, so that probe could never match; it now probes every mesh format the manifest does register.
- Choosing a category in the navigator applies it straight away rather than setting the filter and waiting for `Search`. A category is a filter, and picking one out of a list of counts does not look like it will wait.
- The file list's `Name` column sizes itself to the names on the page in front of you, and `Path` takes the width left over, so names are no longer clipped at a fixed 170 pixels and no empty strip is left past the last column. A column the design sizes to its content or to the remaining width now keeps doing so; a stored pixel width from an earlier session no longer overrides it. The column is also re-measured as each page arrives, because a self-sizing column otherwise only ever grows and one long name early on would leave it oversized for every page after.
- Choosing a category no longer locks the Archive Browser into that category. The navigator applies a role filter, and the category counts were taken over the entries that filter had already accepted, so the list collapsed to whichever category was selected and offered no way back to the others — with 98,966 models in view, an archive of a million-plus entries showed `Model` and nothing else. A facet dimension is now counted against every other filter but never against itself, so the full set of categories and their real counts stay visible while one of them is selected. The list also has an `All` row that releases the filter, and it keeps the selected row across a query instead of clearing it while the filter stayed applied, which had left the filter narrowing every later result with nothing on screen to show for it. Lite exposes no role control of its own, so leaving the view modes that show the navigator now releases the filter as well.
- A folder filter no longer releases itself after one query. Filtering to a folder narrows the result to that folder's contents, so unless files sat directly in it the folder dropped out of the rebuilt folder list and the selection fell back to `All` — the filter applied once and then quietly stopped applying. The active folder is now always present in that list.

- Text that has settled on screen no longer keeps the language it was written in once you change language a second time. The catalogue line under the file list, the item-set banner, and the Item Finder's status and icon warm-up lines each stored the finished sentence rather than how to rebuild it, so the first change re-resolved them correctly and threw away what it needed to do it again: switching to another language and back left the first one's wording there for the rest of the session. The warm-up line was the most stubborn of them, because its summary is kept per archive session and was replayed word for word every time the dialog reopened. The detail pane beside a selected item repaints too, having previously kept whichever language its counts and `None` placeholders were built in.
- The model preview no longer leaves an unpainted black margin when its window moves to a display at another scale. The renderer runs in its own process whose window is reparented into the app; that process defaulted to the WinForms `SystemAware` DPI mode, so it kept measuring in the scale of the monitor it started on and Windows resized the hosted window underneath the host on any other scale. It now declares `PerMonitorV2`, matching the app. The host also paints its own background on `WM_ERASEBKGND` in the renderer's clear colour, because WPF cannot draw inside a hosted window's region and any margin the renderer has not covered yet — mid-resize, mid-DPI-change, or before the first frame — was left unpainted. Resizes now read the host's client rect, the same figure the renderer's own reconciliation timer reads, instead of rounding the layout rectangle a second time and leaving the two disagreeing by a pixel.

- Switching language now re-localizes the file grid and the catalogue line, which previously kept whatever language was active when they were built. Grid cells resolve their labels through value converters bound to the row's own record, so nothing in the row signalled that the language moved, and the settled catalogue line was stored as already-formatted text. Cycling the picker therefore left, say, a Simplified Chinese `模型` in the File type column and a Traditional Chinese count line above a UI that was otherwise Japanese. The behaviour predates the fourteen-language work — English to German did the same — but was easy to miss with three languages and unmissable with fourteen. The catalogue line now keeps its resolver rather than its resolved text, and a language change refreshes the grid view so the converters re-run; the grid selection is preserved across that refresh.
- Sixteen Spanish strings had their accents stripped — `instalacion`, `indice`, `sesion`, `anade`, `Mayus`, and the cache-status badges — and now read correctly.

- Faces are no longer classified as metal. Skin packs its `_sp` map differently from equipment: on a shield or a blade the red channel is a constant 1.0 filler and blue is metalness, but a skin map puts the subsurface term in red and leaves blue saturated — `cd_phm_00_head_0001_sp` measures R 0.770 and B 0.997 flat across the whole face. Read as metalness that made every face 100% metal, and 24 of 72 sampled heads classified metal rather than skin. A metal classification takes the lower ambient floor and the cut diffuse, so a face rendered at 0.45 of the brightness its own albedo carries, grey and waxy. The declared channel layout for a skin surface map now says only what the channel holds, as it already did for hair. Across 1,195 assets the share rendering below half their albedo falls from 1.51% to 0.08%, heads recover from 0.958 to 0.999 of their albedo, and nothing renders worse.
- Metal detail on a part that is not classified as metal no longer loses its light. Where the source supplies a metal map the diffuse term is cut, because a conductor has no diffuse lobe, and the reflection about the reflection vector is meant to replace it. The cut was conditioned on the map and the replacement on the material category, so a part carrying real metalness that classified as something else — a metal boss on a leather shield, studs read as generic — gave up its diffuse and got nothing back. Measured over 1,200 assets against their own unlit albedo, 35% of that group rendered below half the brightness their albedo carries, against 0% for metal that is also classified metal. Both sides now answer to the same condition, weighted by the metal fraction itself, so a mostly-cloth part only takes the reflection where its map says metal. Corpus-wide this halves the share of assets rendering below half their albedo from 4% to 2%; a teal planked shield recovers from 0.20 to 0.84 of its albedo, a woven straw hat from 0.18 to 0.65.
- The preview now reproduces the brightness an asset's own albedo declares. Decoding the visible base textures of 238 weapons and measuring them against their render, an unlifted preview reproduces 0.932 of the brightness the texture carries; the game's own item icons, rendered from these same assets, sit at 0.986 of their source, which settles that reproducing the albedo is the intended target rather than a look. The tone gamma closes that gap to 0.982 for 0.8% of colour reproduction and no change in clipping. It is applied to luminance rather than per channel — a per-channel power raises a low channel proportionally more than a high one and desaturates, which cost 0.958 to 0.747 when tried. An exposure was tried first and rejected: it scales the whole range and pushes the top into clipping, which the view-mode gate caught when the lit pass saturated to match its own base colour. The icons run 1.17x the source saturation; that is their grading and is deliberately not matched.
- Visual-audit captures now open an asset on the camera its preview package declares, the same one the app uses — weapons and shields overhead so a flat face is toward the viewer, helmets and torsos from the front. Captures previously imposed a fixed angle on everything, which showed shields as edge-on slivers at a sixth of their object pixels. Nothing in the app was affected, but measurements taken from those captures were: the brightness calibration above was first fitted against a deficit of 0.850 read off edge-on, grazing surfaces, and overshot.

## [0.8.0] - 2026-07-28

### Added

- Hair and fur now take an anisotropic highlight along the strand instead of a round isotropic one. Crimson ships the strand direction as a two-channel `_f` map in UV space; where one is bound on a hair-family surface the preview evaluates a Kajiya-Kay pair of shifted lobes along it — a sharp near-white primary at the cuticle and a broader secondary carrying the strand's own colour. A single isotropic lobe reads as wet plastic. The highlight only switches where both a strand direction and the hair family agree, because the same binding role also carries screen-space displacement directions that are not strand tangents.
- `SkinnedMeshTear` is no longer an unhandled family. It is a decal shell laid over a head that declares a normal, a surface map and an `_m` atlas whose three colour channels are alternative tear shapes; it has no colour of its own. The atlas now drives coverage rather than being rejected as a technical base, so the shell clips to the tear instead of drawing as an opaque grey card over the face. Its alpha is a broad unrelated field, not coverage, and the preview shows the first of the three shapes because it has no character to choose between them.

### Changed

- Model previews now derive reflectance from the metal fraction instead of from a specular map. Physical dielectrics sit near 0.04 reflectance whatever a packed map claims, and letting that map act as reflectance is what gave cloth, leather and hair a metallic sheen they never have. A source specular map now shapes the metal highlight only, and its influence fades out with the metal fraction.
- Where a submesh binds a real roughness or metal map, that per-texel data is now authoritative over the material-category tables. Those tables are filename and shader-family guesses that exist to keep unclassified parts readable, and they were clamping away measured source values: a metal-classified blade with a leather grip shares one surface map, so a category-wide metal floor erased the split the asset actually authors. A cloth family likewise keeps authored metal for studs, buckles and trim rather than having the whole submesh zeroed.
- Preview normals are now rebuilt from their X and Y channels. Source normal maps are two-channel BC5, so the sampled blue channel is always zero and every normal pointed into the surface.
- The standalone launcher now extracts its verified runtime beneath `%LocalAppData%\CDMW\CDMWArchiveLite\standalone\` instead of a vendor folder named after the author. An existing install re-extracts once on the next launch; the runtime left under the previous path is no longer read and can be deleted.
- Archive indexes are no longer counted against, or evicted by, the size-bounded cache prune. An index is older than every preview it serves, so an oldest-first eviction reached the live index first; index lifetime now belongs to fingerprint supersession instead.

### Fixed

- Metal reflects instead of glowing. A conductor has no diffuse lobe — every photon that leaves it left by reflection — but metal kept a third of the diffuse term, which is large, view-independent and does not vary as the surface curves. The result was polished steel rendering as chalky white plaster: bright everywhere, reflective nowhere. Where the source supplies a metal map the residue now drops to a floor that only keeps a very dark alloy from collapsing between lobes, and the energy it no longer supplies arrives through the environment lobe sampled about the reflection vector, which sweeps as the surface turns. The direct Cook-Torrance lobe also runs at full strength there: it had been scaled to about a third by two constants fitted when a specular map stood in for F0 at ten times the physical value, the same fitting already lifted for dielectrics. Measured over 1,715 metal-bearing weapons and armours the highlight rose from 1.90x the body tone to 2.18x, with the body itself dropping from 0.432 to 0.415 and clipping unchanged. Metal detail on otherwise non-metal pieces — buckles, studs, medallions — reads as metal for the same reason.
- Detail layers now paint in the region their own parameter selects. `_detailDiffuseMaskR`, `G` and `B` name the mask channel that places each layer, but that channel was being overwritten by one read off the mask binding — and `_detailMaskTexture` resolves to a fixed `b`, so all three layers sampled the blue channel. Two of every three detail layers painted in the wrong region and the areas the red and green channels mark received nothing, which is what collapsed fully layered pieces to a single flat tone. The layer parameter is now authoritative and the mask's channel remains the fallback for layers that name none. Across a 1,000-asset sweep 367 assets changed, gaining tonal spread with no added clipping: damask on a mantle, lacing on boots, quilting on a gambeson, and the `hel_00_0354` helmets reading as banded metal instead of one flat dome.
- A torn-edge pattern can no longer become a part's colour. `_tornPatternTexture` selects where a garment is torn; it has no colour, no normal and no surface response, but with no role of its own it fell through to the visible base. On `cd_m0001_00_so_pgm_ub_belt_42008` the shared library pattern it points at became the albedo and the garment rendered as neon green and magenta stripes. It now carries its own role, which keeps the binding on record as evidence while leaving it out of every channel the renderer samples.
- Unfinished authoring can no longer create an effect a part does not have. The `cd_temp_*` placeholders were rejected as a visible base but nothing stopped them reaching the support slots, and `cd_temp_r_m.dds` decodes to `(1, 0, 0)` — so as an emissive map it made a mace's spikes glow orange, and as a height map its red channel drove maximum displacement across a whole surface. Those two slots now reject placeholders outright; normal and specular only demote them, because there a placeholder's absence is equivalent and a real map simply needs to outrank it. Across a 62-asset verification set this cleared every placeholder from the emissive, height and normal slots, and 24 submeshes gained a real normal map in place of `cd_temp_n.dds`. The colour-blending mask is deliberately excluded: there the same texture is a real instruction the game reads the same way, and 252 sampled assets composite through it correctly.
- Emissive surfaces no longer glow red whatever colour they declare. Crimson authors emissive as an intensity mask bound through `_emissiveIntensityTexture`, and every one of 134 sampled `_emi` maps is single-channel BC4. Sampled as RGB such a map reads `(r, 0, 0)`, so multiplying the material's authored emissive colour by it left only the red component. The renderer already had a scalar path for exactly this case; nothing was telling it when to take it, so the emissive texture's format now decides. `cd_phm_02_spear_0052` glows the cyan its material declares instead of red, and the false red cast is gone from a gilded coat's trim, a greekfire thrower and a spear head.
- An `_overlayColorTexture` no longer replaces a part's own colour. An overlay is dirt, wear or a paint pass laid over a base, but it outranked the real base by a wide margin unless the part looked like a torso or a pair of legs — and that slot list never covered gloves, hoods, boots, bags, cloaks or rings. Across 3,148 sampled parts, 223 rendered an `_o` overlay as their albedo, and every one of them had its own `_baseColorTexture` bound alongside it; the overlays are near-neutral grey washes, so those parts lost their colour and their texture. The slot list was standing in for the real question — whether the part supplies a primary base colour of its own texture family — which is now what decides it. Parts whose only alternative is a shared library tile still take the overlay, because there the overlay is the most asset-specific colour they bind.
- Preview contrast no longer crushes the image it is meant to shape. The operator pivoted about 0.5 in linear light, but 97% of a workbench frame sits below that pivot, so instead of raising highlights and lowering shadows it subtracted a near-constant amount from every pixel — and needed a 0.55 floor to stop the darkest third collapsing to black, which is what most surfaces actually hit. Contrast is a perceptual control and now pivots about mid-grey in display space. Measured over a 590-asset equipment sweep this lifted mean rendered brightness from 0.369 to 0.400 without adding any clipping.
- Metal is no longer lit only by its highlights. It has no diffuse lobe, so away from a specular hit its tone comes entirely from wide-angle reflection — but the preview environment concentrates its energy in five narrow softboxes, and nothing supplied that broad component while metal's diffuse path was scaled to a third and floored at 0.24. The environment is now also sampled fully blurred about the normal, tinted by F0 so steel stays steel. Across 590 assets the lighting stage went from darkening metal by 34% to leaving it near parity with every other category, and weapons and shields — the two darkest slots — rose from 0.296 and 0.281 mean brightness to 0.338 and 0.313.
- Authored metal colour reaches the surface again. Two different operations shared one strength: a luma-normalised hue shift that recolours without touching brightness or texture, and a flat luma replacement that reads as paint. Suppressing the second dragged the first down with it, so chromatic metal tints applied at 5% — and 217 of 280 sampled metal parts carry one. Because the shared metal tiles those parts draw from are authored neutral (four of six sampled measure a chroma of 0.001 or less), the sidecar tint is the only thing saying brass, gold or verdigris, and most of it was resolving to grey steel. The hue shift now applies at full authored strength, fading out where the source texture already carries its own chroma so a copper tile is not coloured copper twice.
- Garments are no longer shaped only by their texture. Non-metal surfaces kept as little as 46% of their light-to-shadow range, so a pale cloth sat at 0.90–0.93 everywhere: readable as colour, shapeless as an object. That suppression existed to stop the old contrast operator crushing shadowed cloth to black; with contrast pivoting perceptually the headroom exists, and cloth, leather and generic surfaces now take about 68% of their directional shading.
- A submesh no longer loses its own normal and surface maps to material-wrapper order. Wrapper order says which of a model's materials a submesh uses; it is not a claim that a material's maps belong to one submesh only. Where a model bound the same material to several submeshes, only the one whose local index happened to match the wrapper index accepted its support maps and the rest rendered flat — `cd_phm_00_bag_0035` uses `CD_PHM_04_String_0001` on four submeshes and exactly one got them. Where the binding names the material the mesh declares, identity is already established and wrapper order has nothing left to disambiguate. Across the full 12,340-asset equipment set this brought 227 submeshes out of having no surface response at all.
- Placeholder and library textures can no longer become a part's visible base. The guard that knew about `nonetexture`, `dummytexture` and `cd_common_default_overlay` was only consulted when choosing a colour *layer*, so the primary selector still let them through; it now also rejects `cd_temp_*` unfinished authoring, the shared `cd_texturelayer_damaged_*` wear library, and condition/FX noise. Across the full 12,340-asset equipment set this removed 68 parts that were rendering a placeholder, a temp texture or a wear overlay as their own colour.
- An opacity mask can no longer become a part's visible base. `_overlayColorTexture` on the shared wrinkle material points at `cd_common_00_ub_0001_wrinkle0_opacity.dds`, and the parameter name alone does not say the texture is a coverage mask, so the garment rendered as a black-and-white wrinkle pattern instead of its own colour.
- Emissive surfaces now glow the colour their source declares. Both the preview core and the shader substituted a fixed cyan for every emitter, so greek fire, a lightning thrower and an ancient giant's runes all glowed the same shade. An authored `_emissiveColor` is now read and published, and where a material declares none the multiplier is neutral so the `_emi` map's own colour is what shows.
- Emissive intensity is no longer divided by twelve. That divisor was left in place pending a wider sample of declared `_emissiveIntensity` values; a 535-asset sweep of the shipped archive found 27 emissive batches declaring only 1.0, 4.0 and 0.14. With nothing authored above 4.0, the old divisor put the brightest emitter in the game at 0.33 and the common case at 0.083, which reads as unlit.
- A decal or blend input is no longer eligible to become a part's visible base. Its alpha is the shape of the mark it paints -- damage, scorch, an emblem -- so promoting it to albedo painted the whole surface with the decal sheet including the rectangle around the mark. `_detailDiffuseBlend` pointing at a shared `_dec` library texture had become the albedo of several thrown weapons and a sword, over the layers that hold their real colour.
- A part whose source declares no albedo at all no longer renders in a saturated hue. The per-batch palette that separates submeshes in the untextured view was also published as the base colour, and the renderer shows that colour directly when no base map is bound, so an asset whose textures were never shipped rendered as peach or mauve and read as a broken material. Those parts now publish a neutral grey; the untextured view keeps its palette.
- Where a submesh's response is spread across colour layers, classification now weights each layer by its own coverage, read from the colour-blending mask that selects it, instead of requiring the layers to agree. Across a 201-asset real-archive sweep this lifted the share of parts decided by their own decoded map from 58% to 84% and cut the parts still classified from equipment-slot position from 80 to 17.
- A minority metal region no longer makes a whole part metal. A leather scabbard with a metal chape reads as leather, and its chape keeps its own metal because the shader honours the per-texel map.
- Material classification now reads the surface map instead of inferring metal from the equipment folder a model sits in. That inference called a grimy linen shirt, a red velvet dress, a leopard pelt and a fur hide metal because they are stored beside plate; a map measuring no metal is now positive evidence of a dielectric and withdraws the promotion. Across a 120-asset real-archive sweep, 169 of 301 submeshes are decided by their own decoded map, leaving 44 on the slot-position rules instead of 106.
- A submesh whose surface response only exists per colour layer now gets one, composited from those layers through the same mask that selects their colour. Weighting the layers together instead would pin the whole surface near a constant that describes none of them, so uncovered texels keep the layer beneath. Surface-response coverage across the same sweep rose from 139 to 300 of 301 submeshes.
- Specialised material families -- hair, fur, skin, eye, tooth -- are now read from the part's own mesh names, its visible albedo and its declared shader family, not from every binding pooled onto it. A jacket carrying a fur collar contributes a fur binding to that pool, which classified the cloth body of the same jacket as hair.
- A packed surface map is no longer uploaded and then ignored. Crimson stores roughness in the green channel and metal in the blue channel of its `_sp` maps, and binds them through `_materialTexture` on some shader families and `_specularTexture` on others. Neither route declared those components, so armour, swords and shields rendered entirely from a filename-derived category guess. Hair maps declare roughness alone, matching the hair shaders, which never sample a metal channel.
- A garment's own surface map is no longer discarded as a layer. On SkinnedMeshStandard clothing the unsuffixed `_detailMaterialMask` points at the mesh's own `_sp.dds`, while the channel-suffixed `_detailMaterialMask{R,G,B}` point at the shared `cd_texturelayer_*` tiling library. Penalising both alike handed the material slot to `_colorBlendingMaskTexture`, which selects a colour layer and carries no surface response, so those parts rendered with no roughness or metal at all. Across a 120-asset real-archive sweep this restored per-texel surface response to 43 more parts.
- A `_materialTexture` map whose file is named `_sp.dds` now binds to the material slot rather than the specular one. The file suffix was matched before the authored parameter, which dropped the batch onto the legacy specular-gloss response.
- A `_colorBlendingMaskTexture` is no longer declared as packed occlusion/roughness/metal. It selects which authored colour layer owns a texel, so its weights were being read as surface properties, which is how dyed cloth and leather picked up a metal response.
- The visible base texture is now taken from the authored colour layers rather than a detail overlay. `_grimeDiffuseTexture{R,G,B}` are the three layers the colour-blending mask selects; `_detailDiffuseMask*` are overlays. Ranking detail higher handed the albedo slot to an overlay and the authored colours were never used.
- Mask-selected colour layers now take their tint from `_tintColor{R,G,B}` rather than `_scratchTintColor{R,G,B}`. Scratch is the wear accent for the same channels and carries a low blend strength, so preferring it washed authored colours out toward white and yellow.
- Superseded archive indexes and name caches are now reclaimed instead of kept forever. Both are keyed by content fingerprint, so every game update published a new key and left the previous set on disk with nothing referencing it. Reclamation runs at worker startup and after a rebuild, skips any fingerprint a cached root or an open session still uses, and is abandoned entirely if a root manifest cannot be read.

- An embedded model preview renderer now closes when it reaches the end of its host's standard input, which is what happens when the hosting application ends. The renderer's kill-on-close job object is armed just after launch, so a host that ended inside that window previously left a renderer process running with no host to display it.
- The standalone launcher now places the application it starts in a kill-on-close job object, so force-closing the launcher stops the whole Archive Lite tree instead of leaving it running with no launcher attached.
- A worker that no client ever connects to now stops after thirty seconds instead of waiting for a connection forever. The client arms its own kill-on-close job just after launch, so a client that dies during that window previously left a worker running with no pipe to notice and no window to close.
